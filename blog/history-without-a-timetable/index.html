A catalogue still needs an audience

On this page

  2. Rights travel through the new distribution system
  3. The front desk and the organisation behind it
  4. The paths that survive in memory

When I was young, our family sat together on Saturday evenings to watch Jai Hanuman on Doordarshan, and woke up to Chitrahar on Sundays. When the picture became noisy, one of us (it was always me) went upstairs, climbed onto the roof and adjusted the antenna. A Yagi–Uda antenna, for the electronics geek. No remote, one channel, knobs and a twisted piece of metal on the roof.

By the time I wrote Evolution of AI, and its future in B2B in May 2024, each family member could watch the show they wanted, when they wanted, on a device in their pocket. They could also become a creator. The important change was how people lived around the medium: a shared schedule became individual choices, with new expectations and new ways of reaching an audience.

A transmission slot gives a programme a time and an audience somewhere to gather. An on-demand catalogue loosens that constraint. Yet making the next copy available does not make somebody watch it. The scarce thing has moved. The B2B essay follows that change into the organisation behind a conversational front desk; Radio became Spotify follows the need, habits and institutional memory through the wider transition. Here the economic question is what happens to selection, payment and the work behind easier access.

From a schedule to a selection problem

A broadcast schedule coordinates many viewers around one sequence. An on-demand catalogue permits different sequences, leaving each viewer with the work of finding and choosing. Producing another copy becomes easier; reaching a particular person remains a separate problem. Recommendation, editorial selection, search and the relationship between creator and audience acquire roles that differ from a transmission slot.

The measures follow that change. YouTube distinguishes thumbnail impressions, views, estimated unique viewers and watch time.1 A programme can be widely exposed and scarcely watched, or watched deeply by a smaller audience. These observations describe different aspects of attention. When rewards depend on one of them, people may change how they package and produce their work to meet it. What becomes easy to distribute and what becomes profitable to make can diverge.

Rights travel through the new distribution system

Streaming makes a catalogue accessible through a service, while recordings, compositions and contractual claims remain with their rightsholders. Spotify describes payments to recording and publishing rightsholders, with artists and songwriters paid according to their agreements, rather than through a universal fixed per-stream rate.2 The cost of delivering a copy and the contractual path of its income are therefore separate parts of the arrangement. Cheaper delivery can coexist with concentrated catalogue rights or a new intermediary. The constraint may move toward access to the work, access to the audience, or the ability to retain a share of payment.

Uploading changes who can participate. Earning a livelihood also requires sustained demand and a claim on the resulting income. As copies become cheaper, selection and distribution still shape which work reaches an audience and through which relationship its income returns.

The front desk and the organisation behind it

A conversational front desk raises a related economic possibility. Instead of translating a need into a company's menus, a person states the need and the organisation works out how to meet it. The simpler surface asks more of the business: records must be available, responsibility clear and a request connected to a means of fulfilment. Information, exception handling and commercial relationships become part of the interface's practical reach. The valuable change is the reorganisation that makes the simpler surface useful.

Those complements can support businesses in their own right. An integration service may be valuable while systems remain incompatible. A common standard could remove one part of its function while changing relationships create another. Its duration depends on the work it performs and the alternatives that become available.

The paths that survive in memory

The move from a family television to separate screens makes a change in everyday organisation visible. Household income, networks, rights, devices, preferences and business models changed alongside distribution. To carry a mechanism from that history into another setting, we need to ask which conditions travel with it. A fixed and known audience may face little discovery cost even when production becomes cheap. Different rights can distribute the same revenue differently. Existing infrastructure can permit a faster deployment than a system that first needs years of construction; organisational learning or liability can still slow it down. Failed products and persistent older forms belong in the comparison because they reveal the alternatives a successful path can hide.

For cheaper cognition, the question returns in a new form. Once producing an answer becomes easier, finding the relevant answer, trusting it and acting on it can become more valuable. The firm that supplies those complements may capture income, face new competition or make its own service unnecessary. A catalogue needs an audience; an answer needs a place in someone's work. Following that relationship gives the history something concrete to contribute.

  1. YouTube's account of content metrics, checked 16 November 2026. Definitions and denominators differ by metric and format; they are not interchangeable counts of people or useful attention. ↩

  2. Spotify's royalty guide, checked 16 November 2026, describes the service's payment arrangements. It supports the distinction between delivery, the right to receive payment and the artist's contractual share; it does not establish every streaming market's distribution of income. ↩
