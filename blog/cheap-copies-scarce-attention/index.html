Cheap copies, scarce attention

On this page

  2. The work inside the knowledge good
  3. A smaller audience can become worth serving
  4. Distribution can feed back into the product
  5. Delegating the selection

Making information becomes a different economic activity when producing another version is cheap. A specialist explanation can reach a smaller audience, a creator can explore more alternatives and a useful service can be adapted to a need that once cost too much to serve. The cost reduction opens possibilities while changing where the difficult work lies. Attention, verification and distribution still shape which possibilities become valuable. How do these complements change as production and selection become easier, and who receives the gain?

The Red Pill asks whether reusable intelligence could itself become a unit of exchange. This article follows a different part of that economic problem: how easier production changes selection, distribution and the value of reaching a particular audience.

When making gets cheap, choosing gets harder

Imagine a specialist reader who once chose among ten useful reports and now faces a thousand. The reader's time is unchanged, so inspecting every report is no longer feasible. Search, recommendations, reputation and trusted relationships become part of finding something worth reading. The producer and the selector need not be the same actor. A lower production cost can increase the value of selection, or better filtering can reduce the cost of finding a useful source. The outcome depends on the reader's purpose. Entertainment, a consequential decision and learning a difficult subject place different values on variety, error and depth. A system optimising engagement may perform a different selection from one helping someone make a decision.

The work inside the knowledge good

Fluent text is one part of a useful report. Interviews, experiments, source access, verification and judgement can remain expensive. If checking dominates the cost, faster drafting may produce only a modest saving. If a tool helps compare evidence or locate inconsistencies, its contribution can be larger because it changes another stage of the work.

Suppose, only for illustration, that preparing a specialist report takes one hundred units of active work: forty for research, twenty for drafting and forty for checking. Reducing drafting to two units lowers the whole requirement to eighty-two, not ten. If the same tool also halves the work of finding relevant sources without degrading the result, another twenty units become available. The economic contribution depends on the stages it changes.

Stage Earlier requirement Faster drafting Faster drafting and source finding
Research 40 40 20
Drafting 20 2 2
Checking 40 40 40
Total active work 100 82 62

These are assumed workloads, not product results. They make verification's role explicit rather than placing it inside a vague “human in the loop” surcharge.

Creative production has a similar division. Generating alternatives can make exploration less costly; selection, coherence, rights and a distinctive practice give the result its particular value. A useful account follows that whole process instead of treating the count of generated outputs as completed work.

An explanation and a reusable capability are also different goods. A working support procedure, for example, may carry knowledge of a task, the conditions under which it succeeds and the means of checking its result. Its useful transfer depends on those conditions being met in the receiving setting. Reusing that capability therefore includes adapting its dependencies and checking that its result still means what the recipient needs. Economically, reuse could reduce repeated setup while creating work around adaptation, verification and responsibility. That is a more specific exchange than selling another copy of a description.

A smaller audience can become worth serving

Lower creation cost can make a specialised report or service viable at a smaller scale. This is a real change in the feasible business model: a need too narrow to support a costly production process may sustain a cheaper one. Its income still depends on demand and access. A reader can want the specialised work and lack money or time to buy it. Several producers can discover the same need, competing much of the saving into lower prices. A platform can control access to the audience, while a direct relationship may give the producer another route. Willingness and ability to pay, alternatives, distribution cost and retention determine which of these arrangements lasts. Relevance therefore creates a reason to choose, not a guaranteed margin. Lower cost can benefit the audience even when competition leaves little surplus with any one producer.

Distribution can feed back into the product

Imagine two services making equally useful reports. One already reaches many readers; the other has to find each reader from scratch. If the first can observe which questions recur, which explanations resolve them and why readers leave, its distribution can support faster improvement. Better fit can then bring more readers and more observations. Cheap production amplifies that loop when the service can afford to try more versions between observations.

The loop is conditional. Clicks may reward a provocative title rather than a sound explanation. A large general audience may teach less about a specialist need than a small, direct relationship with the people who have it. More observations also help only if the service can interpret and use them; permitted access to feedback is a different asset from merely serving a large readership. Distribution is an advantage here through the learning it supports, not because size automatically supplies a better product.

That makes a narrow audience interesting in another way. A producer who understands a recurring, costly question can improve around it without first winning a mass attention market. Lower production cost can make that relationship economical. The competing possibilities are a growing incumbent's feedback advantage and a specialist's closer fit, with selection costs and the audience's purposes deciding how either becomes useful.

Delegating the selection

Summarising, filtering and retrieval move part of the selection process into systems. A person might then inspect fewer sources directly while using more of their content. The constraint changes again: source quality, permitted access, objectives and the ability to inspect a consequential conclusion become part of the service.

A producer may optimise for the filter rather than the ultimate reader. What the filter lets through influences whose work becomes visible, just as a publication's editorial policy or a search ranking can. Processing a document also differs from granting it authority: an agent needs a reason to rely on a claim, not merely the ability to extract its sentences.

The economic question follows what useful selection costs, whose purposes it serves and what control the user retains. Delegation can make a wider range of material usable without eliminating the relationships and responsibilities around its use.

Where the saving goes

Cheaper production can widen participation, intensify competition or make an intermediary more important. Effective filters could reduce selection costs; direct relationships could weaken intermediary dependence; better source quality could make the larger supply more useful. These alternatives belong inside the argument about the market, rather than after an assumed concentration outcome. Ownership and competition determine who retains a return from the changing constraints. The measure of useful output determines which part of the gain becomes visible. The finished good is something somebody can use, trust or enjoy. Its economics lies in the work and relationships that make that possible.
