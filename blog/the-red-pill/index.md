# The Red Pill: AI skews the power law. And that's okay\!

Jobs are not a law of nature. Humans made jobs as a framework for exchanging value. Money pays for the work; increasingly, attention determines who gets the opportunity to offer it. But neither arrangement exhausts what we could exchange.

[The Blue Pill](https://rawthoughts.in/blog/the-blue-pill/) (on RawThoughts) scenario starts with a familiar loop. If you already have distribution, you get more deals, more observations and more chances to improve. AI makes the work cheaper and lets you run more experiments through that distribution. The power law was there before AI; AI accelerates it. For the people who put in the work and built their position before this asymmetry arrived, that can be a very good deal. They did build the stage.

For someone starting with little or no distribution, it sounds almost like a doomsday scenario. Right?

Well, welcome to the Matrix, Neo. It's time you realised there is no spoon.

The Red Pill scenario asks about a different economy: one where the thing we exchange is intelligence itself. How would we unlock it, and what could somebody without an enormous audience contribute? First, some context is overdue.

**Note**

**This December 2023 thought experiment describes a possible future for transferable intelligence and the economy built around it, not a working market.**

<a id="still-early-still-closer-than-it-appears"></a>

## Still early, still closer than it appears

My starting assumption is that AI is very early in its development: closer to the vacuum-tube era than the mature transistor era, to borrow an analogy. Even if technical progress accelerates, the change in a business arrives through operations, costs and adoption. The Blue Pill pressure takes time to work through those arrangements.

Gradually is the important word. It is also a dangerous comfort. Humans are not especially good at imagining a change that keeps accelerating. Objects in the mirror are closer than they appear.

In [The ineffable geometry](https://causality.in/blog/the-ineffable-geometry/) (on Causality), I ask whether a richer language for causality could let us understand and localise intelligence inside a model. My ambition there is determinism: an account of why the result follows, beyond the statistical tools on which we currently rely. The continuation explores what such an unfamiliar description might demand of us. These are early mathematical hunches, not established methods for extracting a skill from a neural network.

The part I want to carry into this argument is the possibility of transferring intelligence in pieces. Suppose we could identify a useful capability, extract it, preserve what makes it work and combine it with another. If that premise feels too abstract, read the geometry first and come back. It's in cyberspace now; the article is not going anywhere.

For this thought experiment, let's roll with it.

<a id="displaced-into-what"></a>

## Displaced into what?

The big employment numbers make the stakes obvious and the answer no clearer. McKinsey's 2017 scenarios put 400–800 million people potentially displaced by automation and needing new jobs by 2030. Goldman Sachs's 2023 estimate concerned work equivalent to 300 million full-time jobs exposed to generative-AI automation. Those are different quantities, technologies and scenarios; neither number means that many people have already lost their jobs.<sup><a id="fnref-note-6a6f6273"></a>[1](#fn-note-6a6f6273)</sup>

I am sceptical of the confidence with which such numbers travel. We can paint a number on the problem while disagreeing about what we are measuring, and a hundred things can change between a projection and its outcome. Even after specifying the measure properly, the question remains: displaced into what?

In the Red Pill scenario, the human hours required to deliver an outcome become very small. Humans then take a different role: quality assurance and alignment of the intelligence producing it. That is the possibility I want to explore through two deliberately ordinary businesses.

<a id="first-sell-an-apple"></a>

## First, sell an apple

You sell a product, say an apple, to an Indian bachelor. Everything is fine until you are selling a shit ton of apples to those customers. Now you have support staff, operations and a process that is partly manual and partly technology.

Three problems start to dominate. Support staff join and leave, taking experience with them. Different people give inconsistent answers. And support needs to coordinate with the supply chain without gaining unrestricted access to sensitive customer or company information.

My bet is that AI becomes the cheaper, faster and more consistent way to do this once its actual performance and marginal cost fit the business's timelines and support requirements. That comparison has to include the whole job. A quick answer that creates a refund problem or exposes information has not solved the support problem.

Who ensures the system gives the output you actually need? It is not a deterministic machine that can simply promise the right answer. During the transition, the support staff perform quality assurance on the interaction between AI and customer. They identify the exception, correct the response and help contain operational knowledge in something the next interaction can use.

The apple-support system for Indian bachelors gradually moves from human support towards an AI autopilot. In this scenario, the accumulating checks make the operation more consistent, reduce the disruption caused by staff turnover and preserve the boundaries around information. It becomes the obvious choice when the complete comparison works in its favour.

Think of the pursuit of a 99.99999% accuracy service-level agreement. Each extra nine can demand disproportionate work; “doubling the effort for the next nine” is my shorthand for that difficult tail, not a measured scaling law or an achieved guarantee. That work is the transition. Calling the system AI does not make the transition disappear.

<a id="then-sell-a-banana"></a>

## Then, sell a banana

Your colleague sells a banana to the same kind of customer. Why should they go through all that pain again?

You should be paid for the effort that made apple support work. Your colleague should be able to reach the market faster. In the Red Pill scenario, they borrow the intelligence for “sell X to an Indian bachelor” and work on the banana-specific part.

This assumes we can separate what transfers from what does not. A banana has different properties, complaints and supply arrangements. The transferable capability has to retain its conditions, limits and checks; copying the apple answers would miss the point. The useful unit is the ability to do part of the job, with enough of its surrounding knowledge to make it work elsewhere.

“I know how to sell a banana.” Neo's next download, perhaps.

The Lego intelligence economy, if you will. Extend those two scenarios and start building your Lego land.

[Some things never change](https://daostudio.in/collections/protocols-and-reuse/some-things-never-change/) (on DAO Studio) follows the practical problem inside that premise: what crosses from one setting to another, what must be adapted and how the recipient checks the result. A reusable description is a beginning; a capability that works in the receiving situation is the proposed good.

<a id="a-different-unit-of-exchange"></a>

## A different unit of exchange

Extend the framework far enough and I can imagine decentralised autonomous organisations forming around these pieces. A worker no longer has to work only for a particular company. They might maintain and check one particular intelligence Lego, used by many organisations.

The people with the most relevant skills perform quality assurance and alignment for that piece. A market values its usefulness; use of one capability creates demand for the complementary capabilities around it. A group of people could earn from making a narrowly defined piece of intelligence reliable across the places that need it.

This is why the token matters. When we describe distribution's skewed power law, we are looking at attention on the buying side: who can reach a customer, win trust and make an offer? The Red Pill changes what can be supplied. Instead of selling only an agency engagement or a product, someone contributes to a web of reusable intelligence, checked by people with specific skills.

That would be a different economy of exchange. The valuable token would be intelligence itself. Calling it a token here means a proposed separable unit of capability; it does not establish that a blockchain token, a payment instrument or a market already makes that unit possible.

<a id="early-enough-to-build"></a>

## Early enough to build

If this is the direction of travel, it is not too late. It may be as early as it could be.

Think of businesses collecting data in the early 2000s and 2010s, before their analytical tools could make full use of it. Some of that accumulated history became useful when the tools improved. My suggestion follows the same pattern: build and retain operational intelligence while we work out how to make it transferable. Know what the operation does, where it fails, what the skilled person notices and what makes a result acceptable.

In this scenario, the more specific and separable the useful piece, the sooner someone else can adopt it. A capability that solves an identifiable problem has a place in a network of intelligence. That is what I imagine becoming practical AI for everyone. It is a hypothesis about adoption, not a guarantee that narrowness by itself creates demand or income.

I'll admit it sounds like a fairy tale: free markets and human alignment magically cooperate, freeing us from the shackles of whatever, blah blah blah. There is a lot of work between the premise and that world. We still have to make the pieces transferable, make the quality assurance meaningful and make the exchange reward the people doing it.

But hey, it beats the Blue Pill scenario anyway.

Just remember: objects in the mirror are closer than they appear.
