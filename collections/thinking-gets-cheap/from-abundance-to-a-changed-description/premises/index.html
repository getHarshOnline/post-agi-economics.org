Where does the saving go?

From abundance to a changed description 2 / 3

A service used to cost 100 to produce and now costs 70. Thirty units have become available. Does the customer pay less, does the supplier earn more, or does the saving finance another round of work?

Put a price beside the cost. Before the improvement, one unit sells for 120, costs 100 and leaves 20 with the supplier. Hold quantity and quality fixed for a moment. The new production cost is 70, but that number alone does not determine the new selling price.

Thirty units, two sides of the transaction

Let pp denote the price after the improvement. The customer's saving on the same purchase is 120−p120-p. The supplier's residual rises from 20 to p−70p-70, a gain of p−90p-90. Together:

(120−p)⏟customer saving+(p−90)⏟supplier’s additional residual=30. \underbrace{(120-p)}_{\text{customer saving}} + \underbrace{(p-90)}_{\text{supplier's additional residual}} =30.

The units are hypothetical money amounts for one unchanged service, not measured AI savings. Taxes, financing and additional fixed costs are left outside this first comparison.

Price after the improvement Production cost Supplier's residual Customer saving Increase in supplier's residual
120 70 50 0 30
105 70 35 15 15
90 70 20 30 0

Each row contains the same technology. What differs is the price arrangement. A fixed-price contract leaves the saving initially with the supplier. Full pass-through preserves its old residual and lowers the customer's bill. The middle row shares the immediate benefit.

Competition, bargaining and contractual terms help explain which row resembles an actual transaction. They cannot be inferred from the cost reduction. Nor does the supplier's residual tell us which person ultimately receives it: the business could distribute it, invest it or use it to meet another obligation.

The cost also belonged to someone

The table looks different from the other side of the old expenditure. If all thirty units were wages removed from this service, a worker loses thirty while the transaction becomes cheaper to supply. In the first row the supplier gains that amount. In the last row customers gain it through their lower bills. The former worker need not be the customer receiving the saving.

If instead the improvement reduces electricity use or rework, another set of payments and resources changes. The same cost number can conceal different income effects. That is why a local gain and a household gain require connected accounts, rather than a presumption that the saving reaches everyone equally. Income and prices meet inside the household's budget.

The first transaction is not the whole adjustment

A lower price can bring more customers. A higher residual can fund capacity. Work released from this service can become useful elsewhere, or remain unused. Once quantity responds, thirty units per service is no longer the total gain; once the workflow changes, the cost may include new checking and integration.

Even the quality held fixed here has to survive contact with the task. A faster draft that requires expensive correction may not cost 70 when the complete job is counted. The execution comparison has to establish that part before the economic allocation begins.

This small account keeps the question concrete. We know what the technology changed, what the price rule held fixed and where the immediate difference appeared. To compare worker ownership, public provision or a longer investment path, we need the arrangement around that transaction. The saving has a size. Its destination still has to be explained.
