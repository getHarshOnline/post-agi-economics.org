What changes when the constraint moves?

A workshop needs usable plans and machine time. When plans are scarce, producing them faster can raise output. Once enough plans exist, the machines become the limit. Another plan then adds little to current production, even though its cost has fallen. A production relation that represents both inputs can describe the workshop on either side of this crossing. The answer changes because the limiting input changes. But suppose the workshop changes owners, delegates its purchases or stops recording a distinction we need. Recalculating its output alone will not tell us what those changes do.

Re-solving the production problem

A lower price changes the relative cost of inputs; new capacity changes the feasible set. The constraint can move from planning to machinery, drafting to approval, or producing copies to reaching an audience. Even a sharp change in the solution can be something the model was built to describe. The workshop's outcome depends on the relation among its inputs. With strict complements, unused plans wait for machine time. With substitution, a cheaper input may replace another. A planning improvement could also reduce the machine time needed per job, changing a coefficient rather than simply adding plans. These possibilities call for different accounts of the improvement. Re-solving a model that includes the relevant relation can be enough; the scale of the technology's ambition does not decide whether new formal machinery is necessary.

A decision inside the aggregate

Suppose a firm delegates purchases to two systems. Treating the firm as one decision-maker may remain adequate for total expenditure. It may be inadequate for the compatibility of what was purchased, the permission to buy it or responsibility for an error. Two purchasing systems could each respect a spending limit while jointly ordering incompatible inputs. Another pair could spend the same aggregate amount on a compatible set. The total records expenditure in both cases but loses the relation that determines whether production can proceed. Representing the separate decisions and their coordination becomes useful for that question.

Make the lost distinction explicit. Suppose the systems each buy one component for fifteen units. Components come in two interface types; a pair works only when the types match.

Purchases Total expenditure Can the components work together?
Type A and type A 30 Yes
Type A and type B 30 No

Write the coarse observation as g(x)=30g(x)=30 in both cases, and compatibility as h(x)h(x), equal to one for the first pair and zero for the second. No function of expenditure alone can recover compatibility: it would have to assign both values to the same input. Retaining the two interface types restores the answer in this finite example.

The aggregate still answers the expenditure question. It fails only when asked for the relation it discarded. This elementary loss of information is a useful diagnostic; a general result about when an economic model must be enlarged would require a specified class of models and permitted observations, not just this table.

Who owns the gain is set in the model's closure

The workshop might change who receives its residual income while leaving the production technology untouched. Private ownership, a worker-held claim and a shared public pool can give the same output gain different income and demand consequences. A model with an adjustable ownership rule can compare these arrangements. One that fixes ownership implicitly needs a changed closure before it can answer the same question. The closure specifies what adjusts, what stays fixed, who receives the gain and who bears the loss. It is a different part of the argument from the production relation.

Behaviour can then change in response to the arrangement. People learn, firms enter and customers respond to incentives. A coefficient estimated under the previous conditions may cease to describe the new ones. Re-specifying or re-estimating that relation is the answer when behaviour has changed the relation itself; accounting identities can remain true throughout.

When the thing counted and the way it is counted both change

A survey broadening its definition of AI use can create a jump in a reported series even if no firm changes its workflow that day. The event counted by an adoption rate has moved. Comparability requires a common counted event or a visible break in the series.

Identification poses another problem. Firms adopting a tool may already differ in management, capacity or demand. The observed productivity gap includes those prior differences unless a suitable comparison separates them from the effect of adoption. A meaningful economic object can therefore remain difficult to identify with the available data. The repair follows the problem: a comparable measure, a stronger research design or a narrower estimable claim. Categories and incentives also shape the observation. A changed category can reveal an existing activity, while a reward attached to the measure can change the activity that follows.

When a price approaching zero changes the model itself

As a price approaches zero, a model may continue to describe the activity at increasingly extreme values. A singular limit is more specific: rank, state dimension, regularity or solution class can change, or solving the model and then taking its limit can give a different answer from taking the limit first. The distinction comes from a derivation. It gives the research a way to investigate an actual change in the problem instead of using abundance as a substitute for one. A large economic change and a formal singularity can coincide, but each needs its own argument.

Each frontier moves the others

Capability, affordable access, reliable operation, organisational adoption and institutional response move through different processes. Permission can enable operation; operation can expose a bottleneck; investment can remove it; lower cost can alter demand. The order of crossings feeds back into their timing. A clock for one of these frontiers needs a defined process and an account of the conditions under which the crossing becomes possible; one universal countdown would erase the dependencies that make those times different.
