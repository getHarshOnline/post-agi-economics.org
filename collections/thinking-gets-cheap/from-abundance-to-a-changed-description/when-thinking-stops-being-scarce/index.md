# When thinking stops being the scarce part

Push the cost of cognition towards zero and its available supply towards infinity. What happens to wages and productivity, prices and output, income and consumption, money and its circulation? Economics describes these relationships through ratios, rates and models built for particular conditions. My starting question is what happens when a numerator or denominator approaches an extreme that those conditions never allowed. Which relationships would we have to work out again?

Think of a factory that can produce as many designs as it wants. It still needs energy, equipment and materials to make them. Abundant design may leave its production model intact while moving the constraint to another input. But knowing how much the factory can produce does not yet tell us who receives the income or who can afford its output.

Those are two different ways for the inquiry to develop. We can follow a model into the limit and find that it keeps answering its question. We can also discover that an answer about output leaves out a relationship we need to understand income, purchasing power or the time people have to adapt. Start with the assumptions, then see which kind of change we actually have.

<a id="which-assumption-moved"></a>

## Which assumption moved?

An economic model does not carry every assumption made by the discipline. Its own account has to be examined. Does it depend on scarce skilled judgment, costly search, slow contracting or limited coordination? Does it hold the firm, the employment relationship or the owner of an asset fixed? Does it use a market price to represent an output that might become free, personalised or produced inside a household? Does its estimate depend on behaviour and institutions staying stable long enough to compare?

Cheap cognition can put pressure on each of those assumptions without changing them all at once. A machine can draft a contract quickly while the parties still need time to negotiate it. A design can be copied cheaply while permission to manufacture it remains scarce. A firm can automate a task while leaving its contracts, customers and ownership unchanged. The model needs the particular change that matters to its question.

A new parameter value may call for recalculation. If a different input becomes scarce, we need to follow that input and its income. If an actor or relation is missing, we need to add it to the account. Each changes what we have to do next; taking a quantity to an extreme is how we investigate the model, not a reason to decide the result beforehand.

<a id="more-cognition-the-same-complement"></a>

## More cognition, the same complement

Take a deliberately small production model. Let $C>0$ be cognitive input and $P>0$ an essential complement. In this example, output is

$$
Y(C,P)=\frac{2CP}{C+P}.
$$

This constant-elasticity production function has equal weights and elasticity $1/2$. Choose units for the two inputs within this model: $C$ represents cognitive input and $P$ the particular complement being examined. Applying it to an industry would require defining and measuring those inputs.

Hold $P$ fixed and increase $C$. Dividing the numerator and denominator by $C$ gives

$$
Y(C,P)=\frac{2P}{1+P/C}\longrightarrow 2P.
$$

More cognition still helps, but it cannot replace the complement. At output price one, the marginal values are

$$
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial C}=\frac{2P^2}{(C+P)^2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial P}=\frac{2C^2}{(C+P)^2}.
$$

As cognition becomes abundant, the marginal value of another unit of $C$ approaches zero. The marginal value of another unit of $P$ approaches two. This does not say that thinking is useless: without it the joint production fails. It says that the next unit of an already abundant input contributes less than the next unit of the scarce complement.

Under competitive marginal-product payments, the complement's income share is

$$
\frac{P\,\partial Y/\partial P}{Y}=\frac{C}{C+P}\longrightarrow 1.
$$

The production relation has not broken. It tells us where income moves under its assumptions. Ownership tells us who receives it. A different bargaining or payment rule would require a different calculation. [The workshop example](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/moving-constraints/capital-theory/) follows the same shift with strict complements: once plans are plentiful, machine time limits the next job. Here the smooth production relation also lets us follow the marginal payments.

For $P=1$, the progression is:

![With the complement fixed at one, output rises toward two as cognitive input increases, its marginal contribution falls toward zero, and the complement's income share rises toward one.](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/from-abundance-to-a-changed-description/when-thinking-stops-being-scarce/cognition-and-the-complement.svg)

At $C=1,3,9$, output is $1,3/2,9/5$; cognition's marginal value is $1/2,1/8,1/50$; and the complement's income share is $1/2,3/4,9/10$. The three panels trace those same formulas between the selected points. Income shares assume competitive marginal-product payments at output price one.

<a id="the-same-output-can-reach-different-buyers"></a>

## The same output can reach different buyers

Now hold the production model, quantities and payment rule fixed. Set $C=3$ and $P=1$. Output is $3/2$. The cognitive input receives $3/8$ and the complement receives $9/8$; together those payments exhaust the output.

Suppose the recipient of the cognitive payment stays the same. Compare two possible owners of the complement. One household spends $3/4$ of the income it receives; the other spends $1/4$. Those spending fractions are stipulated for this example. They are not estimates of actual households, and the example does not yet explain saving, prices or a subsequent equilibrium.

Switching the recipient of the complement's payment changes consumption by

$$
\left(\frac34-\frac14\right)\frac98=\frac9{16}.
$$

The inputs, output and total factor payments are identical in the two cases. An account retaining only those aggregates therefore cannot distinguish them. Whatever consumption it predicts from that account, it must return the same answer twice. Since the actual answers are $9/16$ apart, it misses by at least half that gap, $9/32$, in one case. Recording who owns the complement and how that recipient spends recovers the missing distinction.

This is a small version of the question studied in [Compression is a promise about questions](https://causality.in/collections/changing-descriptions/from-summary-to-repair/compression-is-a-promise/) (on Causality). Two situations can look identical through one description and differ in a consequence we care about. Here ordinary ownership and consumption accounting supplies the repair.

It does make the buyer question unavoidable for this comparison. More output and more household purchasing power are different claims. [Who gets to buy what machines produce?](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/productivity-and-purchasing-power/) follows the channels through which people might receive the gains: prices, wages, new work, ownership, transfers and public demand. A channel must be described and tested before its existence is treated as compensation for another channel's loss.

<a id="when-how-and-what-belong-together"></a>

## When, how and what belong together

A calculation at two values of $C$ leaves out the transition between them. Who invests before the complement becomes valuable? Who owns it already? Which temporary businesses appear because deployment is incomplete? Can a firm reverse its choice, and can a household wait for the next source of income? Those questions change which future becomes reachable.

There is no single morning on which every task and institution becomes post-AGI. Capability, access, recurring use and institutional adaptation can cross their thresholds at different times. [One Yardstick, One Clock](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/one-yardstick-one-clock/) asks how comparable execution and a model of transition could make the time available to adapt measurable. That clock remains an instrument to develop and test. The inquiry here develops alongside it: where do constraints and income move, and which description keeps explaining the change?

The history of media makes the interaction easier to see. [Radio became Spotify](https://rawthoughts.in/blog/radio-became-spotify/) (on RawThoughts) follows a need that persists while distribution, habits, measurements and businesses change around it. [A catalogue still needs an audience](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/history-without-a-timetable/) asks which scarce resource earns income after copying becomes cheap. These histories supply mechanisms to investigate. They do not settle an AGI date or prove that the same ownership and demand arrangements will recur.

<a id="what-would-make-the-example-useful-outside-the-page"></a>

## What would make the example useful outside the page?

The next empirical step needs a named production setting, meaningful measures of cognition and its complements, evidence for substitution, a payment rule and observations of who receives and spends the income. Alternative explanations need the same opportunity to fit. If a conventional model answers the question, that is useful progress. If it fails, the failure must say which consequence it lost and what additional information restores it.
