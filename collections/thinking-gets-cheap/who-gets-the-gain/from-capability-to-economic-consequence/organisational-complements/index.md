# The work around the tool

A team reduces report drafting from two days to two hours. The report still waits a week for reconciliation and approval. The impressive component improvement has barely changed when anybody receives the work.

That hypothetical queue makes the organisation around a tool visible. Better source data, a different handoff or clear responsibility for an exception can make faster drafting useful. Calling all of it "adoption lag" hides the work of changing the process. The return depends on which of those investments other tasks can reuse and how long it takes for the savings to cover their cost.

<a id="the-process-can-defeat-the-component"></a>

## The process can defeat the component

If the faster draft creates more discrepancies for reviewers, total work may even grow. The saving changes one stage while reconciliation or approval still limits the system. Removing that limit may require better source data, fewer handoffs, a revised approval rule or an accountable owner for exceptions. Managerial attention, training time and trust become inputs to the improvement, alongside the software. Multiplying the drafting saving by the number of workers would miss both those costs and the remaining queue.

Put the illustrative stages on the same clock, using an eight-hour working day:

| Stage | Earlier process | Faster drafting only | Drafting plus redesigned handoff |
| --- | --: | --: | --: |
| Gather and reconcile inputs | 8 hours | 8 hours | 4 hours |
| Draft | 16 hours | 2 hours | 2 hours |
| Wait for review slot | 40 hours | 40 hours | 8 hours |
| Review and acceptance | 4 hours | 6 hours | 4 hours |
| Elapsed working time | 68 hours | 56 hours | 18 hours |
| Active work across these stages | 28 hours | 16 hours | 10 hours |

These are designed process times, not observations. The middle column assumes a faster draft requires two extra review hours. The last assumes input quality and the review handoff also improve. It does not credit all fifty saved elapsed hours to the drafting tool. Review-slot capacity and reconciliation have changed too.

Elapsed time follows when a recipient can use the report. Active work follows labour consumed by these stages. A shorter queue can matter greatly to the recipient without saving an equal number of worker-hours. Conversely, released labour may not increase output while demand or another stage remains fixed.

<a id="complementary-investment-changes-what-we-observe"></a>

## Complementary investment changes what we observe

[Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson's Productivity J-Curve](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148) examines how general-purpose technologies require complementary intangible investment, including new processes, skills and business models. If some of that investment is poorly measured, the timing of measured productivity can differ from the timing of underlying economic work. A delayed payoff and a failed project can look similar early on. Distinguishing them requires observing the complementary work as well as the resulting process: whether data improves, handoffs change and accepted work becomes faster or less costly. Spending on software and training describes the investment; the changed outcome describes its return.

<a id="readiness-also-selects-who-adopts"></a>

## Readiness also selects who adopts

A capable firm may adopt first because it already has useful data, skilled staff and the ability to redesign a process. Comparing adopters with non-adopters can then mix the effect of the tool with the effect of that pre-existing capacity. The Danish adoption study by [Pulito and coauthors](https://docs.iza.org/dp18515.pdf) finds strong associations with firm capacity and workforce readiness. The result motivates this selection concern; it does not prove that every adopter's productivity gain is caused by those characteristics.

A credible test may use a rollout, a suitable instrument or a comparison with defensible pre-trends. It also has to ask what the treatment contains. A “tool rollout” accompanied by process redesign estimates a package, not necessarily the isolated model.

<a id="scaling-can-reveal-different-complements"></a>

## Scaling can reveal different complements

A small team can resolve exceptions informally. A large deployment may need consistent permissions, monitoring, escalation and incident response. The organisation that works for a pilot may not work for scale. Some complements are reusable: a sound data interface can support several tools. Others are highly specific and can become stranded if the system changes. Treating all integration spending as durable capital misses that difference. The relevant account includes useful work after review, total cost, reliability, learning and the ability to replace a supplier. It also asks whether benefits spread across the organisation or concentrate in teams that already had the best conditions.

<a id="complements-and-their-payoff"></a>

## Complements and their payoff

The complement hypothesis becomes specific when improved data or coordination changes the measured result under a credible comparison. It weakens when checking consumes the saving, redesigned processes yield no material gain, or demand does not value the additional output. [The production relation](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/moving-constraints/capital-theory/) locates the limiting input, while changes in [workers' hours, employment and pay](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/labor-economics/) reveal how the resulting reorganisation reaches them. The same process improvement can support more output or fewer hours, depending on demand and how the work is arranged.

The organisation is doing more than installing a component. It is deciding how the component fits into work, what other work becomes necessary and whether the combined process earns its cost. That is where a local capability becomes an economic change.
