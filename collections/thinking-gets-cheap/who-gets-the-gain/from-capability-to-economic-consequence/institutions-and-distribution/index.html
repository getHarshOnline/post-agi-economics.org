Two economies can have the same technical capacity and distribute its benefits differently. If we want to understand welfare or buyer capacity, ownership and institutions belong inside the argument, not in a paragraph added after a production forecast. Economists call part of this specification a closure: the rules that say what adjusts, what is held fixed, how markets clear and who receives residual income. A closure is necessary to solve a model. It is not necessarily a description of the only possible world.

One production gain, several arrangements

Take a hypothetical service that costs 100 to produce before a new tool and 70 afterward, for unchanged quality and quantity. The saving of 30 gives us a common production change against which to compare different arrangements. Under competitive pass-through, the service's price could fall. Under a protected contract, the supplier could retain much of the saving. Under worker ownership, a residual might reach the workers through their ownership claim rather than their hourly wage. Under public provision, a budget saving might support other services.

A supplier may use the saving to expand, improve quality, service debt or compensate risk. A public budget saving is not automatically paid to households. A lower price helps consumers in proportion to their use, not necessarily in proportion to need. The same cost reduction therefore cannot identify the recipient without describing the surrounding arrangement.

Keep the immediate claim separate from what its recipient later chooses to do:

Arrangement First claim on the benefit Next decision affecting the economy
Competitive pass-through Customers through lower prices Buy more of this service, spend elsewhere or save
Supplier retains the difference Supplier's residual account Invest, distribute, repay an obligation or retain cash
Worker-held residual Workers in their capacity as owners Allocate between distribution and the enterprise's needs
Public provision The relevant public budget Change the service, taxation or another expenditure through its budget process

The rows do not promise any behavioural response. Nor are they mutually exclusive labels for whole economies: a supplier can have worker owners and face strong competition. The comparison specifies a channel and then asks how it operates.

Distribution changes the next production decision

The first-round allocation can affect demand, investment and bargaining. A worker receiving more income may consume differently from an asset owner receiving the same amount. A firm retaining the saving may expand capacity, hold cash or return it to owners. Their choices depend on expectations and constraints, not only accounting. That feedback makes a static allocation incomplete for some purposes. If the question is immediate incidence, a short-run comparison may suffice. If it is the path of demand and capacity, behaviour and adjustment over time matter. A model that originally held ownership and demand fixed can still answer its narrow question. It becomes inadequate when we ask it to compare institutions it never represented. Comparing those institutions requires a change in the model's question or closure.

Governance chooses more than a score

Maximising output, protecting a consumption standard, reducing inequality, preserving meaningful participation and limiting environmental harm are different objectives. A productivity evaluator cannot decide how to trade them off. It can sometimes show the consequences of a chosen rule. It cannot make the rule legitimate simply because it can compute an optimum. A constrained optimisation also inherits the omissions of its objective and constraints. Unmeasured harms do not become acceptable by disappearing from the data. The separation between deciding and executing is thus substantive. Who may change the objective, who may act, who bears losses and who can challenge the decision are institutional questions.

Different arrangements, different paths

A highly automated economy can support strong household demand or concentrate gains while buyers lose income. Public demand, exports, shared ownership, new goods, price adjustment and non-market provision introduce further possibilities. Those channels make buyer capacity a question about the economy's arrangements, not a binary destination selected by its technical capacity. A useful comparison states the resource constraint, funding mechanism, incidence, behavioural response and feasibility of each alternative. It also tests whether the effect survives a simpler explanation such as ordinary competition or a temporary investment cycle.

The household consequences depend on these ownership and adjustment conditions, and on the price arrangement that Where does the saving go? follows. The technology expands a feasible set. Institutions determine who can act within it, receive its gains and challenge its costs.
