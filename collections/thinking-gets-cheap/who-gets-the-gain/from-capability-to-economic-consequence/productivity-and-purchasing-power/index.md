# Who gets to buy what machines produce?

If machines do more of the work, people still need a way to buy what they produce. That is the buyer question behind Post-AGI Economics. Lower prices can help, and so can new work, ownership income or transfers. But they reach different people, under different conditions. A production gain can become a cheaper purchase, greater earnings or a return someone else receives. Follow it into the household's budget to find out which.

<a id="lower-prices-can-help-without-offsetting-every-lost-income"></a>

## Lower prices can help without offsetting every lost income

Consider a hypothetical household that receives 100 units of income, spends 60 on housing and other commitments, and has 40 left for a basket of services. If that basket becomes 20 percent cheaper, the original basket costs 32. The household gains room in its budget. Now suppose its income falls to 80 while those commitments stay at 60. It has only 20 available. The price reduction does not restore the basket. The average productivity gain leaves out the position of someone who loses income while a large part of their expenses does not fall.

| Household budget | Before | Cheaper services | Cheaper services and lower income |
| --- | --: | --: | --: |
| Income | 100 | 100 | 80 |
| Fixed commitments | 60 | 60 | 60 |
| Available for the service basket | 40 | 40 | 20 |
| Cost of the original basket | 40 | 32 | 32 |
| Balance after buying that basket | 0 | 8 | −12 |

For this fixed basket and fixed commitments, an income loss of eight would just exhaust the price saving. A loss of twenty exceeds it. In symbols, with income $Y$, commitments $F$ and basket cost $B$:

$$
\text{room after purchase}=Y-F-B.
$$

The illustration does not say prices or obligations remain fixed in an actual transition. It makes those assumptions visible. Another household may own an asset whose income rises or receive a transfer; the identical service-price reduction then meets a different budget.

The distribution can disappear even when an aggregate account balances. Extend the example to two identical households, each initially earning 100 with commitments of 60 and a basket costing 40. After the change, let the first earn 80 and the second 120, with both baskets now costing 32. Total income remains 200; buying both baskets now costs 64 rather than 80. The aggregate has 16 units to spare after commitments and those purchases. Yet the first household is short by 12 while the second has 28 left. That surplus does not reach the first household merely because it exists in the total. A transfer, shared ownership or another income channel would be an additional arrangement, with its own conditions.

<a id="labour-income-is-one-channel-not-the-whole-budget"></a>

## Labour income is one channel, not the whole budget

A technology can affect hours, wages, employment, entry into occupations and the timing of payments. Household resources may also include profits, dividends, rent, transfers or earnings from other members. Saving and credit can smooth some shocks, but smoothing is different from replacing a lasting income stream. The household is therefore not interchangeable with a worker. A worker-level wage observation omits other members and assets. Aggregate labour income omits its distribution. A growing output total omits both.

Nor is consumer demand the only source of demand. Investment, public spending and exports can support production. New goods may create demand that a fixed product basket misses. These are possible offsets that an argument about weak buyers must examine, not assume away.

<a id="an-income-floor-has-a-level-and-a-denominator"></a>

## An income floor has a level and a denominator

Suppose households consume 80 while total output is 100. If household consumption stays at 80 and output rises to 120, consumption's share falls from 80 percent to about 67 percent. That falling share alone does not establish that households became poorer. The reverse trap is to protect a share without specifying the level, prices or distribution. An unchanged aggregate share can coexist with losses concentrated among particular households.

An income floor must therefore say what it protects: nominal resources, a purchasing-power standard, essential consumption, or a share of income or output. These are different objectives. It must also say how it is funded and how prices, taxes, output and recipients respond. A formula that drops capital income or treats output as fixed has chosen its assumptions, not solved every possible economy. The buyer-rich and buyer-thin outcomes in [One Yardstick, One Clock](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/one-yardstick-one-clock/) describe different distributional possibilities under those conditions. Comparing them requires a stated consumption standard, income channels and price response, rather than treating a change in output share as a conclusion about household welfare.

<a id="demand-can-amplify-a-shock-but-the-chain-needs-evidence"></a>

## Demand can amplify a shock, but the chain needs evidence

If households facing an income loss reduce spending, local firms may lose revenue and hire less. Debt obligations or costly housing can make adjustment harder. But the size and duration depend on which households lose income, what they stop buying, which prices adjust and what other demand replaces theirs. A small saving on digital services may matter little next to housing. A production gain in an exporting firm may reach domestic households through wages or taxes rather than local sales. A capital-intensive provider may reinvest gains for years before distributing them. The relevant time horizon is part of each mechanism.

The strongest evidence would connect deployment and production to the same workers and households, their full resources, prices and expenditure. [Task and firm studies](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/labor-economics/) observe particular parts of that chain: assistance, output, hiring or wages. Following the household requires the links among those observations as well as resources beyond one job.

<a id="transfers-ownership-and-tax-are-different-answers"></a>

## Transfers, ownership and tax are different answers

Concern about buyer capacity does not by itself select a transfer, ownership arrangement or tax rule. Those alternatives differ in administrative feasibility, incentives, funding, risk and who receives the benefit. They also embody choices about what the economy is supposed to achieve. [Different institutional arrangements](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/institutions-and-distribution/) can give the same production improvement different paths into household resources. Output gives the economy something to distribute; income, obligations and prices determine what a household can do with its share.
