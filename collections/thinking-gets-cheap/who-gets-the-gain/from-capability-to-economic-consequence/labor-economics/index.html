The changing work behind an occupation

Two accounting teams do similar work. One has clean records, stable interfaces and a manager able to redesign approvals. The other spends its days reconciling inconsistent documents and finding missing authorisations. An occupation-level exposure score can place them together while the technology reaches their work very differently.

The occupational label hides the work of those two hypothetical teams. Capability reaches tasks through an organisation; changed tasks can alter output, hours, hiring and pay. The evidence becomes much more interesting when we keep those objects in view. An exposure score, a resolved support issue and an entry-level hire each reveal a different part of the movement.

A task description is not a deployment record

Exposure measures compare descriptions of work with an assessment of what a technology could do. That can locate an opportunity or a vulnerability. It does not observe a firm's permissions, data quality, integration costs or willingness to change its process. The two teams' different capacity to reorganise makes this gap substantive, rather than merely a delay before an identical deployment reaches both.

Actual adoption also means several things. A person drafting emails, a team using suggestions in every case, and a system closing cases without human intervention are not interchangeable treatments. Pulito and coauthors' Danish study links surveys to employer-worker records and finds adoption associated with firm capacity, digital infrastructure and workforce composition; exposure measures differ in how well they predict it. That is evidence about who adopts, not an estimate of what adoption does to wages.

Productivity can rise through different routes

A support assistant may help novices recognise a problem more quickly. A planning system may reduce avoidable rework. Automation may remove a task, while new verification work takes its place. Each can raise output per worker through a different mechanism. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's May 2025 journal article follows 5,172 customer-support agents at one business-software firm. Its staggered introduction of assistance raises issues resolved per hour by about 15%, with unequal gains across experience levels.1 It is useful precisely because the work and treatment are specific. It does not estimate an economy-wide employment response.

Aldasoro and coauthors' January 2026 European firm study instead matches survey and financial data and uses US peer adoption as an instrument. It estimates higher labour productivity, consistent with capital deepening, without short-run employment losses; complementary investment and firm size matter. The causal interpretation depends on that instrument's assumptions. Neither the short horizon nor the sampled firms establishes that displacement cannot happen elsewhere or later.

If demand expands, saved time may support more output. If demand is fixed, the same saving may reduce hours or headcount. If the firm improves quality, a quantity measure can miss part of the gain. A task-level result does not select its destination.

A deployment can also change demand for the work. A buyer finding the answer needed to purchase creates a different possibility from completing an existing support case faster. The labour response still depends on the resulting work, its cost and the firm’s decisions.

Little average change can coexist with changing tasks

Humlum and Vestergaard's 13 March 2026 study links surveys in eleven exposed Danish occupations to administrative records through December 2024. In its adopter comparison, differential earnings and recorded-hours effects are near zero, with effects larger than about 2% ruled out over the two years after ChatGPT's launch. Yet tasks change and new oversight work appears.2 A small average difference between adopters and non-adopters is compatible with substantial reorganisation. A change common to both groups is not identified directly by that comparison.

Expectations are another observation. The March 2026 revision of Yotzov and coauthors' firm survey covers almost 6,000 executives in the US, UK, Germany and Australia. Most report little employment or productivity impact over the preceding three years, while expecting larger changes over the next three. These are reports and forecasts, not a randomised estimate of adoption's effect.3 Its October 2026 revision adds countries and a later survey and is a separate observation.

Early-career harm deserves a specific explanation

Lee Tucker's April 2026 analysis finds a marked decline in early-career hiring in highly exposed industry-state cells after ChatGPT. Its broader comparisons also reveal pandemic-era trend changes. A historical decomposition suggests monetary-policy shocks through 2023 could account for up to one quarter of the relative early-career employment decline through the second quarter of 2025. The analysis finds no evidence that those shocks explain the rapid relative decline in hires. That distinction matters when asking what caused the fall in hiring. Exposure is not observed firm deployment, so the study does not directly trace the whole automation mechanism.

Audoly, Guerin and Topa's May 2026 job-posting analysis finds that exposed occupations' relative weakness predates ChatGPT and no clear additional junior-senior divergence. Postings are not completed hires. The studies differ in outcomes, groupings and comparisons; treating one as settling the other would discard the question each can answer.

A firm might reduce junior hiring before deploying a tool because it expects future substitution. It might also reduce hiring because demand weakened. Separating those explanations requires expectations and deployment observations alongside the same firms' hiring, separations and task changes.

A change in the question can change the adoption rate

The April 2026 Bonney and coauthors study separates firm adoption, business-function deployment and worker-task use in the US Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey. It reports 18% of firms using AI in at least one function during November 2025–January 2026, or 32% when weighted by employment.4 Those are different denominators. Neither is the fraction of all work performed autonomously by AI. Changing survey wording also interrupts a simple comparison with the earlier use series; a jump in the reported number can reflect the instrument as well as the economy.

Highfill and Samuels' July 2026 BEA study adds another gap: stated intentions do not straightforwardly predict realised changes in production. It compares expected with actual adoption and separately connects earlier adoption motives to industry accounts.5 The industry account and a worker's task record can both be useful while answering different questions. Joining them requires a defensible relationship between the populations, periods and measures.

Following the gain to the worker

The support study can reveal a mechanism by which assistance helps a novice. The hiring study can reveal a troubling change without observing that mechanism. Connecting them needs evidence on deployment and the same workers or firms, not just a shared date or the word “AI”.

A convincing account connects actual use to quality-adjusted output, full operating cost, prices, hours, employment and pay. It then asks whether the effects persist, spill over to competitors, or shift expenditure between products rather than expand it. The result may be augmentation in one setting and displacement in another. That heterogeneity is the finding to explain, not noise to average out. Even established production gains leave the household's ability to buy the output to be explained. Lower prices, changed hours, wages and income claims can move its purchasing power in different directions; the worker's place in production is one part of that larger account.
