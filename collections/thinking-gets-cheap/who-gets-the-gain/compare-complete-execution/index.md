# Compare complete execution

You want to know whether an agent can prepare invoices for review more economically than a person. Count the whole job: the result, the mistakes, the review and the cost. [One Yardstick](https://post-agi-economics.org/one-yardstick/) develops that comparison between people and agents. Both need a task they can attempt under the same conditions. A measurable objective, a reliable evaluator and access to the right tools make a comparison possible; they do not tell you who will perform better.

<a id="the-same-task-can-call-for-different-actions"></a>

## The same task can call for different actions

Consider invoice preparation. The executor checks an invoice against its order and receipt, then prepares a payment proposal or sends a discrepancy for review. Give both executors the same records and access, and judge them against the same requirements.

**Important**

**The task ends at review.** Releasing funds is outside this comparison.

<a id="when-the-records-agree"></a>

### When the records agree

The invoice requests payment for twelve units. The order and receipt also record twelve. Prepare a proposal with those records for the person who can approve it.

<a id="when-a-discrepancy-remains-unresolved"></a>

### When a discrepancy remains unresolved

The invoice and order record twelve units, but the receipt records ten. Flag the two-unit difference and include the records needed to investigate it. A faster payment proposal would not resolve the discrepancy.

<a id="one-evaluation-applied-to-both"></a>

### One evaluation, applied to both

Agree on four parts of the comparison before the trial:

- **Quality:** did the records warrant each proposal or escalation?
- **Errors:** what did a wrong action or missed exception cost?
- **Human effort:** how much review, correction and escalation remained?
- **Total cost:** what did execution, supporting systems and human effort require?

A correct escalation is useful work. A quick proposal can still be wrong. The evaluator must remain reliable on the cases the executor will actually encounter, including difficult exceptions. [What makes work executable?](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/evaluation-and-execution/) explains that distinction.

<a id="count-the-work-needed-to-reach-an-accepted-result"></a>

## Count the work needed to reach an accepted result

Suppose each executor handles the same 100 invoices. After review and correction, each batch has 100 acceptable proposals or escalations. No funds have been released. These invented figures illustrate the calculation:

| Work or cost for the batch | Human execution | Agent execution |
| --- | --: | --: |
| Human effort performing the initial task | 240 minutes | 0 minutes |
| Human review | 50 minutes | 100 minutes |
| Human correction | 10 minutes | 20 minutes |
| Model and tool charges | USD 10 | USD 20 |
| Accepted results after review and correction | 100 | 100 |

At USD 30 per hour of human effort, the human arrangement costs USD 160: five hours plus USD 10 in tool charges. The agent arrangement costs USD 80: two hours plus USD 20 in model and tool charges. That is USD 1.60 and USD 0.80 per accepted result. The zero in the first row means no human performed the agent's initial step; its machine cost is included in the charges.

The cheaper arrangement still required twice as much review and correction. Keep each initial result beside its reviewed outcome so that the final count does not hide missed discrepancies, unnecessary escalations or correct requests for help. A serious error that breaches a requirement remains a failure even when average cost is low.

**Note**

**The example's cost assumptions.** This example assumes no additional setup or operating expense. A real comparison needs those costs, the intended mix of invoices and an agreed review procedure. Different cases, prices or review effort can reverse the result; the arithmetic is not evidence of either executor's performance.

<a id="adoption-is-a-different-event"></a>

## Adoption is a different event

A successful trial does not show that organisations will use an executor repeatedly. To study adoption, define the population and what counts as recurring use at an acceptable quality and cost. Then observe that event.

One Yardstick proposes a transition clock for the economy. Testing that ambition also requires examining [particular frontiers](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/one-clock-many-frontiers/): a capability becoming accessible, a deployment becoming reliable or an organisation changing its work. These processes need not move at the same pace. A forecast needs an observable process and an [outcome defined before it is known](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/models-and-measures/a-forecast-meets-its-outcome/).

<a id="the-buyers-remain-in-the-picture"></a>

## The buyers remain in the picture

Cheaper execution does not settle who can buy the output. An agent does not need the income with which a displaced worker bought a service. Follow the production gain into prices, wages, ownership and public arrangements to understand how it changes [a household's available resources](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/from-capability-to-economic-consequence/productivity-and-purchasing-power/).

The broader [Post-AGI Economics inquiry](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/) studies those frontiers, moving constraints and income arrangements alongside the clock. Comparing one task contributes evidence about execution. Linking it to an economic transition requires an account of adoption, the work around the tool and where the income goes.
