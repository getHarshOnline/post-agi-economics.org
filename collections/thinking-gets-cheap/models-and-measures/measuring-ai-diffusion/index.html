Counting the spread of AI

Models and measures

Imagine that an adoption survey broadens "AI used in production" to "AI used in any business function". A firm drafting an internal email now qualifies. Its reported category can change while its production process stays exactly as it was.

The broader question reveals uses that were previously invisible and changes what can be compared over time. An increase can come from firms changing how they work, the survey counting more kinds of work, or both. Which event the survey counts, whom it asks and when it asks them all shape the answer.

The question defines the counted event

The US Census Bureau made this change in its Business Trends and Outlook Survey in November 2025, broadening the question from AI used in producing goods or services to AI used in any business function. Its May 2026 account also distinguishes recent use from expected future use.1 The wording change creates a break in what the historical series counts. Comparing the periods requires a common definition where one can be recovered, and a visible break where it cannot. A better current observation can therefore make a simple historical comparison less reliable.

A firm is not a unit of work

Suppose, hypothetically, ten small firms each use one assistant occasionally and one large firm delegates a substantial recurring workflow. A firm-count adoption rate treats each adopter as one. A worker-weighted rate, a task-time rate and a value-added rate can tell different stories. None is inherently the correct rate for every question. Firm counts describe diffusion across firms. Worker weights describe reach across people. A task denominator can describe coverage of work, but requires a definition of tasks and a treatment of assistance versus autonomy. Value weights ask a different question again. A number cannot become the automation share in a production model merely because it is the only frequent series available.

Consider a small constructed survey, separate from the Census observations. All ten responding firms remain unchanged between two questionnaires:

Firm category Firms Qualify as using AI in production Qualify as using AI in any function
Recurring production use 2 2 2
Internal email assistance only 3 0 3
No use 5 0 0
Total 10 2 5

The measured rate rises from 20 to 50 percent solely because the event changes. A bridge questionnaire asking both questions could locate the additional uses. It would not justify multiplying all earlier production-use rates by 2.5: the overlap can change across populations and dates.

Expectations, access and use are different observations

An executive's plan to adopt is not observed adoption. Access to a tool does not show that people use it. Recent use does not show that it completes work at the required quality. Self-reported time saved does not show where the saving went. Yotzov and coauthors' 2026 executive survey separately asks about current use, reported past effects and expected future effects.2 Those categories can disagree without any respondent being inconsistent: firms may expect a larger change than they have experienced. An adoption model that blends them into one state loses that information. It may also confuse anticipation with the effect of deployed technology.

Selection can look like a treatment effect

Firms that adopt may already have better data, more skilled workers or stronger demand. Workers using a tool may be those most able to benefit from it. The difference between users and non-users then includes more than the tool. A survey can describe that selection. It cannot automatically remove it. A causal comparison needs a design whose assumptions address why adoption occurred, alongside a clear definition of the treatment and outcome.

The same caution applies to task exposure. An occupation-level score crosswalked to an industry is several steps away from observed autonomous work in a particular firm. It may be informative, but its grain and intended use should remain visible.

Keeping a series comparable as use changes

A consistent series can follow a local frontier: a defined kind of use among a stated population over time. A survey showing widespread assistance answers a useful question while leaving the coverage of autonomous execution open. Preserving both conclusions makes the evidence available to the worker-outcome inquiry without turning use into an employment effect. A local clock likewise needs a frontier observed consistently enough to time. Definitions and denominators determine which movement the series can describe. They are part of the economic account, not footnotes beneath its headline number.

  1. The Business Trends and Outlook Survey covers US employer businesses, excluding farms. It asks about AI use during the preceding two weeks and expected use over the next six months. The Census Bureau's May 2026 account examines responses collected from 14 December 2025 to 3 May 2026, after the wording changed on 17 November 2025. Its use rates count businesses, not the share of their work performed by AI. ↩

  2. The March 2026 revision of Firm Data on AI draws on nearly 6,000 senior executives reporting about their firms in the US, UK, Germany and Australia between November 2025 and January 2026. It asks which AI technologies the firm uses or intends to use, and separately asks about effects on employment and sales volume per employee over the previous three years and expected effects over the next three. Those effects are executives' reports and expectations. ↩
