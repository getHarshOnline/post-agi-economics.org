# Who captures value when the bottleneck moves?

When one input becomes cheap, the remaining scarce inputs acquire more importance in production. Turning that importance into income requires control over access, a way to charge and a surplus after supply costs. A production constraint, a rent-bearing asset and a financial claim therefore describe different parts of the path from useful input to economic return.

<a id="the-necessary-input-may-have-little-pricing-power"></a>

## The necessary input may have little pricing power

Imagine a digital service that needs a specialised data connection. Without it, the service cannot operate. If several suppliers offer equivalent connections and customers can switch easily, necessity need not produce large margins. Now change the arrangement: one supplier has an exclusive right to the relevant data, and switching requires a long migration. The technical role is similar, but control and outside options differ. The rent story comes from those rights and alternatives, not simply from the word “data”. A physical input can show the same distinction. Installed capacity may be scarce while providers compete for customers. An essential service may have a regulated price. A supplier may face costs or liabilities that absorb the additional revenue.

A small cash-flow comparison makes the difference tangible. In both arrangements, the connection supports the same service. The service brings in 100, and its other operating costs are 60:

| Arrangement | Connection payment | Connection supplier's cost | Connection supplier's residual | Service operator's residual |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| Interchangeable suppliers | 10 | 8 | 2 | 30 |
| Exclusive access contract | 30 | 8 | 22 | 10 |

The amounts illustrate two stipulated contracts, not estimates or an explanation of how market prices settle. Total residual across the supplier and the operator remains 32. The contractual price moves twenty of it from the service operator to the connection supplier.

A larger residual is not automatically economic rent. Some of it may compensate capital, risk or an omitted cost. Likewise, an exclusive contract does not justify any arbitrary price: customers can reduce demand, reorganise the service or find another way to meet their need. Those alternatives decide whether the arrangement survives.

An outside option can bound the price without selecting it. Keep the same service revenue of 100 and other costs of 60, and suppose the operator can earn a residual of 10 through an alternative arrangement. For this connection to be at least as attractive, its residual $100-60-p$ must be at least 10, so its maximum acceptable connection payment is 30. With supplier cost 8 and no other costs or risks, a payment below 8 cannot cover supply. The possible bargain lies between 8 and 30. The two contracts in the table occupy different points in that interval.

Nothing in that interval explains which price is agreed. Competing equivalent suppliers, the buyer's credible ability to switch, negotiation and the terms fixing future prices add the price-formation mechanism. A better outside option can lower the ceiling; an unreliable substitute may leave it high. The bottleneck supplies an opportunity to bargain, while the alternatives and contract determine how its surplus is divided.

<a id="follow-the-right-not-merely-the-asset"></a>

## Follow the right, not merely the asset

Owning a piece of equipment, operating it, holding a contract for its output and owning a company that supplies it confer different claims. A contract can reserve capacity without granting a right to all future scarcity income. A provider can control an interface while licensing the underlying asset from someone else. These distinctions matter when a capability improvement changes demand. Who can raise the price? Who bears expansion cost? Who can substitute? Who has promised a fixed service level? Which party receives the saving? The same technical bottleneck can support different incidence under different contracts. Describing an industry's “winners” without those relationships risks attaching the benefit to the most visible name rather than its recipient.

<a id="new-supply-can-remove-a-transitional-rent"></a>

## New supply can remove a transitional rent

A shortage can invite entry, expansion or substitution. Higher prices may finance the capacity that later weakens those prices. A supplier that succeeds in removing a constraint can help its customers while diminishing its own scarcity advantage. There are also persistent constraints. Some locations, rights or coordination arrangements may be difficult to reproduce. Persistence still needs an explanation: high fixed costs, a legal limit, learning, network effects, customer switching costs or another mechanism. The horizon therefore belongs inside the claim. A useful bridge during a transition may not remain a toll road afterward.

<a id="cheap-capability-can-coexist-with-concentrated-dependence"></a>

## Cheap capability can coexist with concentrated dependence

A user may have access to inexpensive outputs while depending on a small set of providers. Low marginal cost and a competitive retail price do not establish a resilient supply arrangement. The [Financial Stability Board's 2024 AI assessment](https://www.fsb.org/2024/11/fsb-assesses-the-financial-stability-implications-of-artificial-intelligence/) identifies third-party dependence, provider concentration and correlated behaviour among financial-sector vulnerabilities. It does not prove that every AI market concentrates. It gives a concrete reason to examine shared infrastructure and failure channels separately from price. Open weights, portability or more providers could weaken a rent. Conversely, proprietary workflows or scarce access could preserve it even as a model's price falls. Both are countercases a serious claim has to face.

<a id="the-duration-of-a-rent"></a>

## The duration of a rent

A rent weakens as substitution becomes feasible, competition dissipates the surplus, customers retain more of the saving or additional capacity outruns demand. Its contractual basis matters just as much: the party operating a necessary input may lack the claim through which the surplus is paid. [The production constraint](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/moving-constraints/capital-theory/) helps explain why an input matters; [information markets](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/cheap-copies-scarce-attention/) show how selection and attention can create a different scarcity from installed equipment. The durable-income question follows control, alternatives and time together. A moving bottleneck creates an opportunity to supply something useful. Whether that opportunity becomes a lasting return depends on the relationships around the supply, not just its place in the technical system.
