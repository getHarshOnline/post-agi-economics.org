Where the constraint moves

A workshop has five usable plans and machine time for ten jobs. Better planning gives it twenty plans, but the machines still carry only ten. The workshop has gained a real capability, and its next useful investment has changed.

This deliberately hypothetical picture is a way into the economics of cheaper cognition. Plans, designs and coordination can become easier to supply while equipment, energy, access or human presence still bounds the result. Following the constraint lets us ask what expands, who can supply the next missing input and which account of production continues to describe the activity.

A workshop limited by planning and by machine time

Give each completed job one unit of planning and one unit of machine time. The workshop's capacity is then the smaller of its planning capacity and machine capacity. With five planning units and ten machine units, it can complete five jobs. Raising planning to twenty permits ten, not twenty. The same relation describes both cases. At first an extra planning unit can help; once the machine is full, more planning cannot increase output unless it improves the process or frees machine time. A falling price of planning does not erase the machine constraint. This is a model of strict complements, not an estimate of an industry. Real production may allow substitution, alternative techniques or quality changes. A planning improvement might reduce the machine time each job needs, changing a coefficient rather than simply increasing one input. The mechanism has to identify which of these claims it makes.

With planning capacity aa, machine capacity mm and one unit of each per job, the relation is:

y(a,m)=min⁡{a,m}. y(a,m)=\min\{a,m\}.

At the corner a=ma=m, adding only a plan leaves output unchanged; adding one unit of both inputs permits another job. This is why a single smooth marginal-product story needs care at the crossing.

After planning reaches twenty, raising machine capacity from ten to eleven adds one possible job. The production value of that increment depends on the job's value and on whether anyone wants it. The relation locates a feasible improvement; it does not fix a supplier's price or establish a profitable investment.

Substitution and complementary demand

Where tasks are substitutes, an organisation may replace expensive work with cheaper work. Where they are complements, it may demand more of the remaining input. A hospital's scheduling, a factory's maintenance and a purely digital service need not share the same production relation. Even a highly digital activity can have a scarce complement: permission to use relevant data, a distribution channel, an accountable signatory, a customer relationship or the capacity to verify unusual cases. These are not all physical constraints. They may be contractual or institutional, and some can change when organisations redesign their work.

Acemoglu's task-based macroeconomic analysis makes the coverage of affected tasks and the size of their cost savings central to aggregate productivity. A dramatic saving on a narrow slice does not automatically become a dramatic saving for the whole economy.

The constraint can move more than once

Increasing machine capacity may reveal a shortage of operators. Training operators may reveal weak demand. Growing demand may reveal scarce working capital. A static ranking of “the AI bottlenecks” misses this sequence. That also explains why a temporary complement need not be a durable winner. A firm can command a high price while supply is scarce and lose that margin after entry. Integration work can be essential during a transition and become routine later. A component's present importance does not establish its long-run bargaining position. The horizon changes the available alternatives. What binds during a pilot may differ from what binds at scale, after investment, entry or a redesigned process makes another input available. A scenario needs those adjustment times alongside its present capacities.

Scarcity, rent and return are three questions

A scarce resource has a marginal value within a production problem. Its owner captures a rent only if contracts, competition and access allow that value to become income. An investor earns a return only after the price paid and the available claim on that income are considered. An essential network might be competitively priced or regulated. A supplier might spend its additional revenue expanding capacity. A customer may own the complementary asset and retain most of the saving. Saying “this input is necessary” resolves none of those cases.

The workshop's limiting input moves within the same production relation. If the question turns to who owns the machines and receives their income, or how separately delegated purchases fit together, that relation alone no longer answers it. The model's purpose determines which change matters: adding planning capacity calls for a new solution, while asking about income claims or coordinated decisions calls for the relationships that make those consequences intelligible.
