# One Yardstick

“Agents don't buy jeans.” Better execution raises a question beyond what a machine can do: who can buy the result? One Yardstick connects a common measure of human and agent execution to the time available for an economy to adapt, and to the way its gains reach households.

[One Yardstick, One Clock](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/one-yardstick-one-clock/) develops the full argument, from a common test of execution to the buyer question. It is a living thesis. The proposed transition clock and the inquiry into moving constraints and rents develop alongside each other. The clock has no empirical forecast yet. [A worked invoice comparison](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/who-gets-the-gain/compare-complete-execution/) gives the first part of the argument something concrete to measure.

<a id="from-useful-execution-to-economic-change"></a>

## From useful execution to economic change

A better answer is only the beginning. Useful work needs a meaningful evaluation, the means to act and an operating cost someone can sustain. Repeated use can then change the organisation of work, the value of scarce inputs and who receives the gains.

## Follow the work into the economy

A trial can tell you whether an agent met the requirements for one job. It takes different observations to tell whether firms will use it every week and whether households benefit when they do.

1. 
   
   <a id="define-useful-work"></a>
   
   ### Define useful work
   
   Choose a task and the result that matters. Include ordinary cases and exceptions, the information and tools available, and who decides whether the result is acceptable.

2. 
   
   <a id="compare-complete-execution"></a>
   
   ### Compare complete execution
   
   Evaluate human and agent execution on common conditions. Count consequential mistakes, correction and review effort, and the resources needed to complete the work.

3. 
   
   <a id="follow-recurring-use"></a>
   
   ### Follow recurring use
   
   Examine what makes the capability economical to adopt, which constraints move and how the gain reaches households through prices, wages, ownership or public arrangements.

<a id="questions-about-one-yardstick"></a>

## Questions about One Yardstick

**What does the yardstick measure?**

Execution of a defined task under common conditions: the usefulness of the result, consequential mistakes, human effort and complete operating cost. The evaluator must remain meaningful for the cases encountered. Defining a yardstick makes a capability test possible; it does not establish in advance who will perform better.

**Can an escalation count as useful work?**

Yes, when the records warrant it. Preparing a proposal and escalating an unresolved discrepancy are judged under the same task, including accepted work, consequential errors, review effort and complete operating cost.

**Why does the framework follow household income?**

More productive execution changes who is paid and what becomes cheaper. Households may receive the gains through lower prices, labour income, ownership or public arrangements. Following those channels connects the technical comparison to the economy in which the resulting goods and services must be used.

**How do capability and adoption differ?**

Capability concerns whether the work can be done under the stated conditions. Adoption also depends on dependable recurring use, integration, switching costs and whether the complete arrangement is economical. Comparing execution makes those questions more precise; adoption needs its own evidence.

**What is the transition clock for?**

The clock asks how the surrounding economy changes, how long people have to adapt and which economy that change leads to, where the yardstick compares execution on one task. It proposes following economic regimes towards agent-dominant execution, distinguishing an economy whose households retain purchasing power from one whose buyers lose it. [The time between frontiers](https://post-agi-economics.org/blog/one-clock-many-frontiers/) explains what such a clock needs, and [A forecast meets its outcome](https://post-agi-economics.org/collections/thinking-gets-cheap/models-and-measures/a-forecast-meets-its-outcome/) shows how a dated forecast is scored against what happened. Beside the clock, the wider [Post-AGI Economics inquiry](https://post-agi-economics.org/about/) asks where constraints and rents move and how the arrangements around production change.
